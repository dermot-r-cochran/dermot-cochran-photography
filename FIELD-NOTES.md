# Field notes — camera settings that worked, per situation

Practical settings for the D3100, kept separate from `CLAUDE.md` (site
conventions) and `STYLE.md` (taste). Each entry is a situation, the settings
agreed for it, and why. Add a line under an entry when a day in the field
confirms or corrects it, rather than starting a new note.

## Woodland floor macro on a support — 70 mm macro, D3100

Written 4 September 2026 for Massy's Wood the following day; the same
settings apply to any fungi, bark and moss work under a beech canopy.

**Support.** A microbead neck cushion on a kneeling mat is the main support:
the lens barrel sits in the curve just behind the focus ring, the two arms stop
it rolling, and it gets the lens lower than any small tripod. Press the lens
into the beads, let go, watch the live-view magnifier for a second to confirm
nothing drifts, then fire. To reframe, lift the whole camera and reshape the
cushion; don't slide it. The small tripod is the backup for anything higher up
or angled at a bracket — legs splayed flat on their lowest catch, sections
unextended, centre column shortened or inverted.

**Exposure.**

| Subject | Mode | ISO | Shutter floor | Aperture |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fungi, bark, moss, still ground | A | 100, Auto ISO off | none — 1/4 s to 4 s is fine on a support | f/8 |
| Low stems and leaves that stir | A | Auto ISO, max 800, min shutter 1/125 s | 1/125 s | f/8 |
| A fly on a cap, anything that walks | A | Auto ISO, max 800 | 1/250 s | f/8 |

- **f/8, and f/11 only when a modest-magnification subject really needs the
  depth.** The 14 MP sensor's pixel pitch is about 5.1 µm, so diffraction
  softens from f/11 and is obvious by f/16. At 1:1 the effective aperture
  doubles — f/11 set is f/22 effective, the same f/22 that cost the lakeshore
  (`STYLE.md`). Depth of field at f/8 is about 1.3 mm at 1:1 and about 5 mm
  at 1:3; shoot from further back and crop rather than stop down.
- **ISO 100 on a support.** 400 is fine, 800 acceptable, 1600 shows chroma
  noise that the vivid picture control amplifies. The support removes the
  shake reason for raising ISO, so let the shutter run long on still subjects.
- **Auto ISO only for moving subjects.** Set it so the camera stays at 100
  until the shutter would drop under the floor, then raises ISO instead. If it
  reaches 800 and still can't make the floor, wait for a lull rather than go
  higher. Check the shutter in the viewfinder before each frame.
- **Focus stacking was considered and dropped.** The D3100 has no focus
  bracketing, so it is a manual sequence in manual exposure, easily ruined by
  a passing cloud or a stirring leaf. Not worth it. Put the focal plane on the
  cap edge and gills (or the cap surface from above), set the sensor parallel
  to a bracket or a tiered row, and let the rest fall away.

**Fire from live view with the 2 s self-timer or the ML-L3 remote.** The
D3100 has no mirror lock-up or exposure delay mode, and mirror slap blurs
frames between 1/15 s and 1/2 s — doubled at 1:1 because the subject is
magnified too. Live view keeps the mirror up, so only the shutter moves.
Manual focus with the live-view magnifier at 100% on the exact point; the
viewfinder is too small and dim for a millimetre of depth. Any lens
stabiliser off on a support. Live view drains the battery: bring the spare.

**White balance under beech.** The floor is lit through green leaves and Auto
under-corrects green, so moss glows and pale caps lean green, vivid makes it
worse. Start on Cloudy with one or two steps of magenta on the fine-tune grid
and check the first frame against the moss and a cap; preset manual off a grey
card is the accurate alternative, at the risk of a floor that looks lit in a
studio (fine-tune a step towards amber if so). Set it once under the canopy and
leave it so the album holds together. Shoot RAW+JPEG — the site builds from the
JPEGs, so the setting matters, but the raw is the way back — and put a grey
card in the first frame of each set-up.

**Light.** Bright overcast is the light for the floor, not sun: a beech canopy
in sun throws hard dapple the sensor can't hold. Shoot under the cloud and wait
out the breaks. Gusts overhead move the sunspots even when the subject is
still. The best window on a breezy day is mid-to-late afternoon when the wind
drops and the sun comes in sideways. Showers are a gift — wet caps and
saturated moss, and the softest light of the day straight after. Inside a
mature beech wood the floor is nearly still while the canopy roars, so a
force 5–6 forecast rules out only the canopy and the wood edge.

**For the photographer.** The screen doesn't tilt, so ground-level work means
lying down: kneeling mat or a bin bag. A right-angle finder is the one
accessory that would make it comfortable.

## Bodies and lenses — why the next body is a D7200, not a D7000

Settled 4 September 2026, after asking whether a D7000 was worth buying for
the spare lenses. The spares are an AF-S 50 mm (already fully working on the
D3100) and a Nikon AF 75-240mm f/4.5-5.6D, which is manual-only on the D3100
because that body has no focus motor. Reviving the 75-240 alone does not
justify a body — it is a slow consumer zoom and a much better telephoto is
already in the bag — so the case rests on what a 7000-series body does for the
macro work and the Tamron 18-400.

**What any 7000-series body fixes for macro.** Mirror lock-up and exposure
delay mode, which the live-view-and-timer habit above exists to work around; a
real pentaprism at 100% coverage, so manual focus through the viewfinder is
usable; AF fine-tune for the fly-on-the-cap shots where autofocus is used; and
Auto ISO with a shutter floor. An older Sigma EX DG macro without HSM, or a
Tamron 90 mm without its built-in motor, is manual-only on the D3100 and
autofocuses on any of them.

**What the D7200 adds that the D7000 does not**, and why it is the one to buy:

- **51-point autofocus with 15 cross-type** against the D7000's 39 and 9 (the
  D3100 has 11 and 1). On the f/6.3 end of the 18-400 in flat light that is
  the difference between hunting and locking.
- **24 MP with no anti-aliasing filter** against 16 MP, which is barely more
  than the D3100's 14. Real cropping room at 400 mm and for crop-and-shoot
  macro; it will also show the 18-400's softness at the long end plainly.
- **About two stops of high-ISO headroom** over the D3100 against the D7000's
  one. At f/6.3 under an Irish sky ISO 800–1600 arrives fast.
- A deeper buffer, 6 fps, weather sealing, and no known focus-drift or
  sensor-oil history to test for.

**Prices at the time of writing:** a D7000 under about €150, a D7200 at
€300–400, the D7100 between. The D7000 is worth it only if the price is close
to nothing and the body tests clean (a few frames at f/1.8 for left-side
focus-point drift and oil spots on the sensor).

**The two-body split once the D7200 arrives.** The D3100 stays in service as a
second body with the AF-S 50 mm f/1.8G mounted — light, fast at f/1.8 for the
dim woodland floor and for portraits at 75 mm equivalent, and fully working on
that body. The D7200 carries the 18-400 or the macro. Keeping a lens on each
means no lens changes in the wet and no dust on either sensor; when the macro
goes on the D7200 the 50 mm stays where it is. The 75-240 stays on the shelf:
it would autofocus on the D7200, but the 18-400 covers its whole range with
stabilisation, so it has no slot. **Not worth selling** (settled the same
day): it fetches €25–50 and moves slowly, since it only autofocuses on bodies
with a motor, so the hour of listing and posting is worth more than the lens.
It was the first lens, it costs nothing to keep, and on the D7200 it is a
working 360 mm equivalent again — a light backup if the 18-400 is ever in for
repair. If the shelf space genuinely matters, give it to someone starting out
with a D7000-class body rather than sell it. Two bodies means two chargers the night before — EN-EL14 for the
D3100, EN-EL15 for the D7200; the SD cards are the same.
